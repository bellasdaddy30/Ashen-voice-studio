CHAPTER 11

The Mark That Wasn’t Ours

They found the first forged notch on the eighth day.

It was in a narrow cut between two dye-houses that had opened overnight and already started to close at the edges. Two clean marks sat at the base of the left wall, cut at the same depth and angle Ninsun used. Beside them someone had added a third—slightly deeper, the edges still sharp with stone dust.

Balag saw it first. He stopped so suddenly that Enhedu nearly walked into his back.

“That one’s not ours.”

Ninsun crouched and studied the three marks without touching them. After a long moment she shook her head. “The third isn’t mine. The angle’s wrong. Whoever cut it was stronger in the wrist and less patient.”

Enhedu exhaled through her nose. “So either we’re not the only ones marking, or the fucking city learned the system.”

No one liked either version.

Zik ran his thumb along the third notch. The stone dust came away clean. Fresh. Maybe an hour old. Maybe less. He looked up the short passage. It ended in the usual blank wall that would probably be ordinary brick again by evening.

“We leave it,” he said.

Balag frowned. “Just walk away?”

“We mark our own and we move. If we start chasing every cut that isn’t ours, we’ll still be here when the streets forget our names.”

Ninsun carefully added the location to her ugly, incomplete map, noting the forged third mark with a small cross. Her handwriting had gotten smaller and tighter over the last few days, as if she were trying to fit more city onto less paper.

They backed out. Behind them the passage had already narrowed enough that a man would have to turn sideways to enter. By the time they reached the main street it looked like a shallow service recess any local would have sworn had always been there.

That night the consequences arrived.

A boy from the lower market found them at the cheap room they were using. He couldn’t have been more than fourteen, all elbows and caution. He held out a folded scrap of parchment like it might bite.

“Man said to give this to the ones cutting marks on the walls,” he said. “Said you’d know.”

Balag crouched until he was eye level with the boy.

“What man?”

The boy shrugged.

“Didn’t say.”

“What did he look like?”

Another shrug.

“Old.”

“How old?”

The boy frowned as though the question itself was unreasonable.

“Older than me.”

Enhedu snorted.

“Helpful.”

Balag reached into his pouch and produced a small bronze coin.

“Try again.”

The boy looked at the coin.

Then at Balag.

“He had clean hands.”

Silence.

Balag lowered the coin.

“What?”

“That’s what I noticed.”

The boy scratched behind one ear.

“Everyone around here has dirty hands.”

He held up his own fingers.

The nails were packed with dust.

“The man didn’t.”

Ninsun looked up from the table.

“A scribe.”

“Maybe.”

The boy shook his head.

“He didn’t walk like one.”

“Temple?”

“I don’t know.”

“Merchant?”

“I don’t know.”

“Soldier?”

“I don’t know.”

The boy looked genuinely frustrated now.

“He stood where the alley bends.”

“Waiting?”

“Like he already knew I’d come.”

Balag exchanged a glance with Zik.

“Did he watch us?”

The boy nodded immediately.

“He knew which room.”

Zik’s expression didn’t change.

“Did you tell him?”

“No.”

“He guessed?”

“He pointed.”

“Before you came upstairs?”

“Before I crossed the street.”

Nobody spoke.

The boy finally accepted the coin.

“He also said something else.”

Balag stiffened.

“What?”

The boy frowned, trying to remember.

“'Tell them…'”

He looked at the ceiling.

“'…they’ve started making the wrong marks.'”

He shrugged.

“Didn’t make much sense.”

He hurried down the stairs before anyone could stop him.

Balag remained at the doorway until the footsteps disappeared.

Then he quietly slid the bolt across the door.

He didn’t wait for payment. He was already gone when Balag stepped into the lane to look for him.

Zik unfolded the scrap.

One line, written in a neat, anonymous hand:

STOP MARKING WHAT DOES NOT BELONG TO YOU.

No signature. No threat beyond the words themselves. The parchment was the same cheap stock the lower scribes used for temporary notes.

Zik turned the parchment over.

Blank.

He held it toward the lamp.

Nothing showed through.

Enhedu reached for it.

“The handwriting.”

“What about it?”

“It isn’t hurried.”

She studied the strokes.

“Whoever wrote this expected we’d read it.”

Balag frowned.

“People usually expect that from letters.”

“No.”

Enhedu tapped one corner.

“They expect we’ll understand.”

Ninsun stood and crossed the room.

She took the parchment without asking.

For nearly a minute she said nothing.

Then she reached into her satchel and removed the map.

She laid the warning beside it.

Zik watched her eyes move between the two.

“The spacing.”

“What spacing?” Balag asked.

“The margins.”

She pointed.

“The left margin matches mine.”

“So?”

“So does the line height.”

Balag blinked.

“I feel like scholars are inventing reasons to be frightened.”

Ninsun ignored him.

“I leave exactly this much room at the top of every page.”

She demonstrated with her finger.

“So does whoever wrote this.”

“Lots of people do.”

“No.”

She looked up.

“Not by accident.”

The room grew very quiet.

Zik picked up the note again.

“You think someone copied your habits.”

“I think…”

She hesitated.

“…someone has seen enough of my work to imitate it.”

Enhedu folded her arms.

“Or you’ve seen theirs.”

Ninsun didn’t answer.

That silence worried Zik more than anything she could have said.

Enhedu read it over his shoulder. “Someone’s territorial.”

“Or the city hired better messengers,” Balag said.

Ninsun took the scrap and set it beside her map without comment. After a moment she drew a small, precise box around the entire western dye-house district and wrote a single word inside it:

CLAIMED.

Zik watched her work. The journal was still in his pack. Four days had become five. He had not opened it. The decision still felt like the only part of this place he actually controlled.

He flexed his left hand inside the linen. The mark was quiet.

Outside, somewhere in the lower districts, another passage was probably opening. Or closing. Or deciding which marks it would allow to remain.

They had drawn a line on the city.

Somewhere, someone had noticed.

Whether that someone lived above the streets or beneath them, none of the four could yet say.

But for the first time since opening the tomb, they no longer felt like explorers.

They felt observed.
