CHAPTER 35

The Lie He Told

Ninsun caught up within a dozen paces.

She did not run and she did not shout. She simply walked alongside him, her presence a weight he had not asked for and could not put down.

The corridor stretched ahead, narrow and oppressive. Torchlight died against the darkness before it could reach the walls.

Zik kept his eyes fixed forward.

“You lied,” Ninsun said.

Her voice contained no anger. That was worse. It was flat and clinical, the voice of someone examining a wound and deciding, with professional detachment, how deep it went and whether it would scar.

Zik kept walking. “I haven’t said anything.”

“You read the journal. You closed it. You decided what the rest of us were allowed to know.” She placed each word carefully. “That is a lie of a different kind. You have never once refused to show me the words before.”

He stopped.

The journal was warm against his fingers when he pulled it free. The mark under the linen on his palm pulsed once, slow and deliberate, as if the account were taking notice of the intention itself.

He intended to open it. He intended to end the accusation with evidence.

He did not open it.

Ninsun watched the decision form on his face.

“You’re still deciding,” she said. “Even now.”

Balag’s voice carried from ahead. “If the two of you are going to stand there arguing about paper, the rest of us are moving.”

Enhedu did not look back. She simply continued walking, the figurine turning once in her hand and then forgotten again.

Zik returned the journal to the satchel. The weight of the decision settled differently this time—not the clean absence of a payment already made, but the heavier, unfinished pressure of a payment deferred.

They walked in silence until the corridor forked.

Three identical passages opened ahead, each sloping downward at the same angle, each breathing with the same slow rhythm. The torchlight revealed nothing that distinguished one from the others.

Ninsun stepped forward and studied the stone above each opening. When she began to copy one of the incomplete symbols, the mark on her parchment went blank under the stylus.

“They’re the same,” she said. “Or the City wants us to believe they’re the same.”

Enhedu looked at the three openings with the remote attention she now gave anything that could not be priced. “Then we pick one and stop standing here.”

Balag shook his head. “Last time we picked, the walls rearranged themselves behind us. I’d rather know which one is the trap.”

“They’re all traps,” Ninsun said. “The only question is which trap the City prefers us to choose.”

Zik opened the journal.

The page that had been blank a moment earlier now held a single line in the hand that was neither his father’s nor his own:

THE LEFT PATH BELONGS TO THE ONE WHO WAS NEVER SENT.

He read it twice. The sentence did not say the path was safe. It did not say the path was dangerous. It only assigned ownership.

He closed the book before anyone else could see it.

Ninsun was watching his face. “What does it say?”

He could have told her. He could have shown her the page. He could have treated the information as shared rather than private.

He looked at the three openings, at Balag waiting for direction, at Enhedu already half-turned toward the left-hand passage because someone else would eventually declare one of them relevant.

He converted ownership into safety because safety was the word that would make the others follow.

“It says the left path is safe.”

Ninsun stared at him.

For a moment he thought she might argue, might reach for the journal again, might demand he open it and prove the accusation one way or the other.

Then she simply looked away, and something in her posture folded inward, the way paper folds when it has finally given up resisting the crease.

Zik turned toward the left-hand passage.

He did not look back.

Behind him Ninsun remained standing for a long moment before her footsteps finally started again—quieter now, more careful, as though she had learned something about the ground beneath her that she had not known before.

Zik reached the mouth of the left-hand passage and stopped because some remaining part of him expected Ninsun beside him.

A heartbeat later he could not remember why he had stopped.

He looked back.

Ninsun was still there, a dozen paces behind him, her arms wrapped around herself against a cold that had nothing to do with temperature.

He knew her name.

Then he walked on.
