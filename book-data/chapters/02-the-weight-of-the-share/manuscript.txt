CHAPTER 2

The Weight of the Share

They left the tomb at dusk.

The desert wind had already begun erasing their tracks. Sand slid into the open stairway behind them in soft continuous sheets, as if the ground itself preferred the hole filled. Balag walked ahead with the two remaining donkeys, spear across his shoulders. Enhedu kept to the side, knife loose in her hand, eyes on the darkening ridges. Zik brought up the rear, the journal and the compass both heavy against his ribs.

No one spoke for the first hour.

The donkeys noticed before any of them admitted it.

The lead animal slowed every few hundred paces, ears twitching as though waiting for the familiar whistle that always came from somewhere behind the line. Kurash had been the one to soothe them when they grew stubborn. He talked to them more than he talked to people, inventing long stories they could not possibly understand.

Now the lead donkey stopped altogether.

Balag sighed through his nose and walked back.

“Come on.”

The animal planted its feet.

Balag tugged the lead rope.

Nothing.

Enhedu watched for a moment.

“It keeps looking behind us.”

“It smells him.”

“No.”

She shook her head.

“It smells that he’s gone.”

Balag gave the donkey another patient pull.

“I liked you better when you only corrected my archaeology.”

“I’ve never corrected your archaeology.”

“You’ve corrected everything else.”

That earned the faintest curl at the corner of her mouth.

The expression vanished almost immediately.

Balag rested his forehead briefly against the donkey’s neck before trying again.

“Stubborn beast.”

“It learned from Kurash,” Zik said quietly.

The words surprised even him.

Balag looked over his shoulder.

“So it did.”

This time the donkey started walking without another protest.

For several minutes only the sound of hooves disturbed the desert.

Finally Enhedu spoke.

“My brother used to whistle.”

Neither man answered.

“He couldn’t carry a tune to save his life.”

Her eyes remained on the horizon.

“My mother always knew he was home because every dog in the neighborhood started barking.”

Another stretch of silence.

“He died before he turned twenty.”

Balag glanced sideways.

“You’ve never mentioned him.”

“There hasn’t been a reason.”

“And now?”

She shrugged.

“There still isn’t.”

“But Kurash reminded you.”

She considered lying.

Instead she nodded once.

“He reminded me that people disappear faster than habits do.”

No one had an answer for that.

The wind shifted.

Behind them, another ribbon of sand spilled over the ridge where the tomb lay buried once more.

Kurash’s absence sat between them like a fourth set of footprints that refused to fade. Every gust left fewer places where Kurash had ever walked.

When they finally stopped, Balag dropped his gear and began checking the animals’ hooves with the same methodical care he gave every practical task. Enhedu crouched a short distance away and cleaned dried blood from under her nails with the tip of her knife. The motion was precise, almost delicate.

Zik sat with his back against a low outcrop and stared at the linen wrapping on his left hand. The mark beneath it was quiet for the first time since the tomb. He did not trust the quiet.

Balag finished with the donkeys and came to sit across from him. The big man did not look at the journal.

“You counting the share already?” Balag asked.

Zik glanced up. “What?”

“Kurash’s share. You’re sitting there doing the arithmetic in your head. I’ve seen that face before.”

Zik almost denied it. Then he shrugged. “If I don’t, somebody else will.”

The words came out harder than he meant. He heard how cold they sounded and hated himself for a second.

Balag just grunted. “Someone always does. Doesn’t mean I have to like watching it.” He was quiet for a few breaths, then added, almost to the sand, “First crew I worked on, we lost a boy before we even reached the quarry. Fifteen years old. Same hungry look. Same fucking math.” He shook his head once. “Still don’t like the sound of it.”

Enhedu’s knife paused. “He would have done the same to any of us if the numbers came out that way.”

Zik looked at her. “That supposed to make it easier?”

“No. It’s supposed to make it true.”

They sat with that for a time. The desert cooled around them. Somewhere far off a night bird called once and did not call again.

Balag eventually stood and checked the straps on the packs a second time, even though nothing had shifted. Zik recognized the habit. The big man trusted his hands more than he trusted silence.

“We’ll reach the city in two days if the weather holds,” Balag said. “Collector’s going to notice the missing man.”

“Let him notice,” Zik answered.

Enhedu wiped the last of the blood from her blade and slid it home. “He’ll notice the extra silver more.”

Zik almost smiled. Almost.

He opened the journal instead.

The last page was still waiting. The three lines in his father’s hand had not changed. The ink no longer looked wet, but the paper itself felt slightly warmer than the desert night should have allowed.

He closed it again before anything new could appear.

They slept in shifts. Zik took the middle watch. He spent it staring at the dark and listening for the low vibration they had heard beneath the tomb. It did not come. The mark on his palm stayed quiet. The compass, when he checked it, still pointed straight down.

Toward morning Enhedu relieved him. She sat without speaking for a long time, turning a small copper figurine between her fingers the way other people turned prayer tokens. Time had polished its smiling face nearly smooth.

“You going to tell the Collector everything?” she asked eventually.

“No.”

“Good.” She tossed the figurine once and caught it. “Everything has a price. Some prices are better left unpaid.”

Zik did not answer. He lay down, pulled his cloak over his face, and tried not to dream of water that wrote.

They broke camp at first light and continued toward Uruk.

Behind them, the last trace of Kurash’s footprints disappeared under the morning wind.
