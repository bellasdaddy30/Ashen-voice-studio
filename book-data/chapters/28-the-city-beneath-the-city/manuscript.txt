CHAPTER 28

The City Beneath the City

They did not return to the plaza, and the City did not force them to.

Instead it simply rearranged the streets until every corridor they chose led back to the same narrow alcove. Zik noticed the first repetition when he saw the cracked tile under the covered walkway for the second time. Balag noticed it when the same dripping water resumed its patient count. Ninsun stopped writing after the third return and simply stared at the wall as though the stone itself had insulted her.

The man who had refused still sat where they had left him, knees drawn up, humming under his breath. He did not look surprised to see them again.

“You tried the eastern passage,” he said. It was not a question.

Balag’s axe rested across his shoulders. “We are walking in circles.”

“No,” the man answered. “You are being returned. Circles would be kinder.”

Enhedu’s knife was already in her hand. “How many times does it do this?”

The man smiled with half his mouth. “Until you stop trying to leave the way you came, or until you decide the price of staying is lower than the price of another attempt. Most people choose the second option before the fifth return.”

Zik looked down the narrowing street they had just walked. The geometry had already begun to shift again; the far end no longer matched the memory of the last attempt. The mark under the linen on his palm pulsed once, slow and deliberate, as if confirming a ledger entry.

They tried a fourth time anyway.

This passage led behind the low wall of inventories and down a slope that grew colder with every step. The air tasted of iron and old paper. For several minutes the City allowed them the illusion of progress. Then the floor leveled, the walls widened, and the same alcove waited at the end of the corridor. The man was still humming.

Balag lowered the axe and sat on the paving stones without ceremony. “I don’t like this place.”

“That is sensible,” the man said.

“I wasn’t asking.”

“The living rarely do.”

Ninsun had already moved to the long panel of wedge marks that ran along the wall opposite the alcove. She brushed dust from the deepest lines with careful fingers. The torchlight sank into the oily stone and did not return.

“These are older than the household inventories,” she said. “Different hand. Different purpose.”

Zik stepped closer. The mark on his palm had begun to warm in a way that no longer felt like warning. It felt like recognition.

Ninsun’s stylus hovered above the first line. “The root forms are familiar. Settlement. Binding. Debt. But the combinations refuse ordinary translation. The first line could mean discovered. Or summoned. Those are not the same thing.”

“No,” Zik said.

She continued, voice growing quieter. “The people who sealed the upper city did not build what lies beneath it. The earliest records claim the first settlers entered from above and found roads, wells, storage chambers already waiting. They believed the place had been abandoned.”

Balag looked at the man in the alcove. “By whom?”

Ninsun’s finger stopped on a character whose edges had eroded. “The word can mean inhabitants. It can also mean remains. Echoes. The dead.”

The man gave a dry, unused laugh. “I preferred it when we were only lost.”

Enhedu’s eyes stayed on him. “You know this language.”

“I know what it cost.”

Ninsun forced herself farther along the wall. The next section was more formal, the wedges deeper and more carefully arranged. Phrases repeated with bureaucratic insistence: the agreement, the binding, the debt below.

“Debt is not exact,” she said. “Obligation. Price. Something owed to the foundation.”

Zik studied the spiral-drop that appeared among the characters. “You once told me it was a royal emblem.”

“I was wrong.” Ninsun’s voice had gone flat. “It is a claim of ownership. Not chosen. Assigned.”

The man in the alcove spoke before she could continue. “I know what it means when the mark chooses someone.”

Zik turned. “Then say it.”

“You would not believe me.”

“Try.”

The man’s cloudy eyes held his. “You are already doing what your father did. He believed knowing the truth would make him free of it.”

The words landed with the weight of an unpaid account. Zik said nothing. He had spent twelve years carrying the shape of that belief; hearing it spoken by a stranger did not make it lighter.

Ninsun reached the final section. The characters here had been cut with force rather than patience. Several surrounding blocks were cracked. She read the first line once, then again, then stopped breathing.

“What does it say?” Balag asked.

She looked at Zik. Whatever he saw in her face drained the last of the anger from his expression.

“The First City was not the first city.”

Silence settled over the alcove the way dust settles after a collapse.

Then the stone moved.

A thin groove appeared beneath the final line and carved itself into the black rock without tool or hand. Another line formed beside it. Then another. The City was writing—or correcting.

Ninsun translated as the words appeared.

“The first city was built over the second.”

A new line.

“The second was built over the third.”

Her breathing quickened.

“The third was built over the—”

She stopped.

Zik stepped closer. “Over what?”

Ninsun stared at the final symbol. “The word is present. But there is no place beneath it. No chamber. No city. No depth. Nothing the language is willing to name.”

The man in the alcove spoke softly. “Do not ask what lies beneath the last city.”

Zik turned. “Why?”

For the first time the man’s composure cracked. His eyes shifted toward the darkness beyond the alcove, and something that resembled genuine fear moved across the wasted face.

“Because it may hear you.”

The first knock sounded from somewhere beyond the chamber.

Three measured strikes. Not loud. Not hurried. The sound traveled through the black stone without echoing, through the soles of their feet and the bones of their bodies.

Ninsun turned slowly toward Zik. He was staring at his left hand.

The mark beneath the linen pulsed—not with his heartbeat. The spiral and the drop had darkened until they were almost black. Beneath the symbol the flesh of his palm began to rise. A thin line appeared under the skin, pushing upward from somewhere deeper than muscle or bone.

Then the line moved.

A single knock sounded against the inside of his palm.

The man closed his eyes.

Ninsun’s voice dropped to a whisper. “The knocks are not coming from the walls anymore.”

Zik looked at her. “Then where?”

She pointed at his hand.

The mark knocked again.

This time the answer came from far below the City—faint at first, easily mistaken for settling stone or the ordinary shifting of ancient foundations. Then it came again. Three measured strikes.

From somewhere beneath them.

Zik’s face had gone completely still.

The man opened his eyes.

And somewhere far below the First City, something knocked back.
