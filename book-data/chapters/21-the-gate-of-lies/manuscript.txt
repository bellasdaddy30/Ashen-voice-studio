CHAPTER 21

The Gate of Lies

They started walking.

For the first hour, Uruk remained behind them as a black interruption in the western sky.

The city wall followed at their backs, visible whenever the land flattened and disappearing whenever the old irrigation ridges rose between them and the torches. From this distance, the watch fires looked pinned to the darkness. They did not seem to move, though Zik knew guards were pacing above the bricks, looking outward for enemies and inward for citizens who had discovered unofficial uses for drainage channels.

The ground beyond the wall was not yet desert. Abandoned fields lay beneath the darkness, divided by dry canals and low embankments. Thorn scrub crowded the old channels. Rainwater gathered in the deeper ruts, turning dust into mud that clung to their sandals.

Balag walked behind Zik, breathing heavily beneath his pack.

“How far?”

“You asked that already.”

“I disliked the answer.”

“Another half league.”

“You said that half a league ago.”

“No. I said we would not stop until sunrise.”

“That was worse.”

Ninsun stepped over a collapsed section of canal wall and nearly lost her footing. Balag caught the back of her satchel before she went down.

She twisted around.

“Careful.”

“I was.”

“You almost fell into a ditch.”

“I identified the ditch before entering it.”

“With your foot.”

“An archaeologist uses the instruments available.”

Balag released the satchel.

“Next time, use your eyes.”

Enhedu moved ahead of them, barely visible except when she crossed a strip of open ground. She had removed the pale wrapping from her hair and replaced it with dark cloth. Mud had dulled the lower half of her robes. One hand remained close to her knife.

She stopped at the top of an embankment and raised two fingers.

The others halted.

Zik listened.

The night carried small sounds farther than daylight did: water dripping from reeds, insects clicking beneath the thorn, Balag’s breathing, Ninsun trying not to breathe loudly enough for Balag to comment on it.

Beyond those sounds came the soft creak of leather.

Then a snort.

Balag’s hand moved toward his axe.

Enhedu crouched, peered down the far side of the embankment, and looked back at Zik.

“You arranged animals?”

“Two.”

“You did not mention animals.”

“You did not ask how I intended to cross the desert carrying four lamps, a hammer, two ropes, and Ninsun’s private library.”

Ninsun adjusted the satchel against her chest.

“It is not a library.”

“Libraries usually have fewer tablets.”

Balag climbed the embankment beside Enhedu.

Below them stood the ruins of a small field shelter. Three mud-brick walls remained. The fourth had collapsed into the dry canal. A roof of bundled reeds sagged above one corner.

Two donkeys waited beneath it.

One was gray, narrow-faced, and watching the group with immediate suspicion. The other was brown and already attempting to chew through the rope that secured it to a post.

Balag stared at them.

“You left animals outside the city wall.”

“I paid a farmer to leave them.”

“Which farmer?”

“A reliable one.”

Enhedu looked around the abandoned fields.

“There is no farm.”

“He is reliable because he understands distance.”

Balag descended the embankment.

The gray donkey flattened its ears at him.

He stopped.

“You selected this one?”

“It selected itself.”

“It hates me.”

“You have that effect on thoughtful animals.”

Balag held out one hand and spoke softly.

The donkey considered him, then allowed him to touch its muzzle.

Ninsun climbed down after him.

“When did you arrange this?”

“Before we entered the archive.”

“You expected to flee Uruk?”

“I expected Uruk to become inconvenient.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is in my work.”

Enhedu entered the shelter and examined the shadows beneath the remaining roof. She checked behind the fallen bricks, beneath the reed matting, and inside a cracked storage jar.

“No farmer.”

“He was instructed not to wait.”

“No footprints either.”

“The rain.”

“The ground beneath the roof is dry.”

Zik looked down.

The animals had churned the dirt around the post. Their hoofprints overlapped in circles. Beyond those marks, the floor of the shelter remained smooth.

No sandal prints led in.

No prints led away.

Balag ran his hand along the gray donkey’s flank.

“They have been fed.”

A bundle of barley stalks lay between the animals. Half had been eaten.

Ninsun crouched beside it.

“The cut ends are fresh.”

“How fresh?” Zik asked.

“Hours.”

Enhedu looked toward the dark fields.

“Your farmer came without touching the ground?”

“He was always light on his feet.”

“That was weak,” Balag said.

“I am tired.”

“That has improved nothing.”

Zik moved to the brown donkey and untied the rope. The animal immediately tried to bite the bronze fitting on his satchel.

He pushed its nose away.

“Do that again and you carry Balag.”

The donkey looked at Balag, then stopped.

Balag pointed at it.

“Thoughtful.”

They lowered their packs.

Balag inspected every strap, knot, blanket, and buckle before allowing anyone to load the animals. He found one split leather loop, replaced it with cord, then found fault with the cord and replaced that with a strip cut from his spare belt.

Enhedu leaned against the broken wall and watched the fields.

Ninsun stood near Zik with Bel-iddin’s warning folded inside her robe.

“You did not tell us about the animals.”

“You mentioned that.”

“You also did not tell us about the seal until the Collector placed it on the floor.”

“I had not known about the seal.”

“The journal, then.”

Zik tightened a strap around a waterskin.

“What about it?”

“You have not told us everything it wrote.”

“I told you about the map.”

“You told us enough to make following you seem slightly less irrational.”

“That was generous.”

“The exact words matter.”

“Sometimes.”

“They always matter.”

“That is why archives are full of people arguing over marks made by accountants.”

Ninsun stepped into his path when he reached for the next pack.

“The journal named you.”

Zik stopped.

Behind her, Balag pretended to be occupied with a saddle blanket. His hands had gone still.

Enhedu continued watching the fields, though her head had turned enough to hear them.

“The Collector named me,” Zik said.

“The journal reacted.”

“It moves whenever it dislikes the conversation.”

“It knocked after he called you Bel-iddin’s successor.”

“It also knocked inside a wall.”

“That is not an argument against meaning.”

“It is an argument against expecting clarity.”

Ninsun lowered her voice.

“What else did your father write?”

Zik looked at her.

The darkness beneath her eyes had deepened since the archive. She had copied the map until her fingers cramped, then packed the copies as carefully as if they were royal decrees. Mud streaked the hem of her robe. A loose strand of hair had stuck to her cheek.

She had followed him through a wall, across the roofs, beneath the city, and into fields where animals appeared without footprints.

She still expected honest documentation.

Zik almost admired the stubbornness.

“He wrote that he was wrong about the depth,” Zik said.

“We know that.”

“And about who was looking.”

“We know that too.”

“That is everything.”

Ninsun watched him.

The journal pressed once against Zik’s ribs.

Slow.

Deliberate.

Her eyes dropped to the satchel.

“That was poorly timed.”

“It has no manners.”

“What did it say after we left the city?”

“Nothing.”

The journal pressed again.

Ninsun folded her arms.

Balag resumed pulling at a strap that required no adjustment.

Enhedu spoke without turning.

“You should lie more carefully around objects that contradict you.”

Zik put both hands on the brown donkey’s pack frame.

“It did not write.”

“What did it do?” Ninsun asked.

“Knocked.”

“How?”

“Once. Then twice. Then once.”

“Beneath the wall?”

“Yes.”

“And something below the drainage channel answered with the same pattern.”

Zik looked toward Balag.

Balag tied off the strap and avoided his eyes.

Ninsun’s face changed.

Not fear. Not yet.

Recognition moving toward fear.

“Four.”

“What?” Enhedu asked.

“The sequence totaled four strikes.”

Balag looked up.

“And?”

Ninsun opened her satchel and began searching through it.

“Do not unpack the tablets,” Balag said.

“I need one.”

“You need all of them. That is the problem.”

She pulled free a narrow clay copy wrapped in linen.

Balag looked at Zik.

“You knew about this?”

“I have stopped asking what she carries.”

Ninsun unwrapped the tablet and held it close to the faint starlight.

“This is from the lower archive. A funerary fragment from the Old Kings period.”

“Funerary?” Balag asked.

“That was the classification.”

“You disagree.”

“I think the classification was convenient.”

Enhedu left the shelter’s edge and approached.

“What does it say?”

“Not enough. The beginning is broken. The surviving portion describes a descent through seven gates.”

Balag gave a quiet sound in his throat.

“My grandfather told stories about seven gates.”

“Most cultures have threshold myths.”

“My grandfather called them warnings.”

“Most warnings become myths after enough people ignore them.”

Zik secured the last waterskin.

“What did each gate do?”

Ninsun ran her thumb above the copied signs without touching them.

“The traveler surrendered something at each threshold.”

“What sort of thing?”

“At the first gate, a covering.”

“Clothing?” Enhedu asked.

“Possibly. At the second, ornament. At the third, rank. The fourth passage is damaged.”

Balag looked toward the drainage channel hidden beyond the fields.

“Four knocks.”

Ninsun nodded.

“The surviving sign can mean name, declaration, or recognized identity.”

Zik touched the pouch beneath his robe.

The bronze seal lay cold against his chest.

“It tells the door what stands before it,” he said.

Ninsun looked at him.

“The Collector’s words.”

“The seal has seven lines.”

“And an empty center.”

Enhedu held out her hand.

“Show me.”

Zik did not move.

“You examined it already.”

“I examined it in a room where a man had climbed away with his hands pointing in the wrong direction. I was distracted.”

“You seemed focused.”

“I can be both.”

Zik drew the pouch from beneath his robe and removed the bronze seal.

The lines remained dark where they had changed beneath his marked palm. Mud had dried around the rim, but the grooves were clean.

Ninsun took it.

She turned it toward the east, where the first thin suggestion of dawn had begun to separate the sky from the land.

“Seven descending lines,” she said. “Seven thresholds.”

Balag came closer.

“And the empty center?”

“The place beyond them.”

“Or the thing they surround,” Enhedu said.

Ninsun looked at her.

“Yes.”

Balag rubbed his beard.

“My grandfather’s version had no clothing.”

“What did it take?” Zik asked.

“First gate took a man’s shadow. Second took the voice people knew him by. Third took the hand he trusted. After that, the story changed depending on how much beer my grandfather had.”

“And the fourth?”

Balag’s mouth tightened.

“His name.”

The gray donkey shifted beside him.

Its hoof struck the ground four times.

Once.

Then twice.

Then once.

No one spoke.

Balag took hold of the animal’s halter.

“It has mud in its shoe.”

“Donkeys do not have shoes,” Ninsun said.

“Then it has mud in whatever you call the part I need to blame.”

Enhedu looked toward the fields again.

“The story ends how?”

Balag stroked the animal’s neck until it settled.

“The man reaches the seventh gate.”

“And?”

“He has nothing left that the gate can take.”

“That sounds useful,” Zik said.

“No. It means the gate takes what is left.”

The eastern horizon grew paler.

Shapes emerged from the darkness: dead canals, thorn clusters, the low swells of abandoned fields. Farther east, the cultivated ground thinned into scrub. Beyond that waited the open desert.

Ninsun handed the seal back to Zik.

“The lower archive contains other fragments. Some say clothing and jewelry. Some say memory, title, ancestry. One version says the final gate requires the living traveler to surrender the last thing that separates the living from the dead.”

Balag glanced at her.

“Breath?”

“The text does not say.”

“Soul?” Enhedu asked.

“That word is not present.”

“Good. I was concerned it might become specific.”

“The translations may describe ritual disarmament,” Ninsun continued. “A ruler entering sacred space is stripped of rank and visible authority. Each gate removes a social identity.”

“Until the ruler is only a person,” Zik said.

“Possibly.”

“And then the last gate kills him.”

“Possibly.”

Balag gave Zik a flat look.

“You sound pleased.”

“I am relieved the ancient priests may have been murdering kings instead of feeding doors.”

“That is the lowest standard for relief I have heard.”

Enhedu rested one hand on the hilt of her knife.

“What if it is not ritual?”

Ninsun looked toward the east.

“Then the stories are instructions.”

“For entering?”

Her gaze shifted to the seal in Zik’s hand.

“Or for preventing something from leaving.”

The wind moved across the fields.

It came from the west, carrying the smell of wet brick, smoke, dung fires, and river mud.

Uruk.

Zik turned.

The city had emerged behind them with the dawn.

It rose in layers above the wall, rooftops pressed close together, smoke already unwinding from ovens and workshops. The temple platforms caught the first light. Their upper walls burned red while the lower districts remained blue with shadow.

From here, no cracks showed.

No false doors.

No rain climbing walls.

No narrow figure standing between torches.

Uruk looked ancient enough to have invented permanence and arrogant enough to tax anyone who copied it.

Ninsun came to stand beside him.

“The texts call the place beyond the gates a city.”

“The First City,” Balag said.

“Not necessarily.”

Zik looked at her.

“You crossed a wall, a roof, a drainage passage, and three miles of mud to correct us now?”

“The term translated as city can also mean ordered enclosure, inhabited foundation, or place under rule.”

Enhedu joined them.

“Can it mean tomb?”

“Sometimes.”

“Prison?”

Ninsun hesitated.

“Yes.”

Balag looked east.

“And lid?”

Another pause.

“Yes.”

Zik felt the bronze seal warming in his palm.

The Collector’s voice returned to him.

The City is not a tomb.

Zik had dismissed the rest as a threat shaped to fit an old fear.

But the old texts had been written before the Collector. Before Bel-iddin. Before Kurash raised the hammer over the black floor.

Ninsun spoke more quietly.

“The accounts do not describe people building the First City.”

“What do they describe?” Zik asked.

“Closing it.”

The gray donkey snorted behind them.

A bird called from the reeds near the dry canal.

The ordinary sound made the silence afterward worse.

Balag looked at the ruined shelter.

“If this is a gate, where is the door?”

Ninsun considered the fields, the hidden drainage passage, and Uruk beyond them.

“Perhaps behind us.”

Enhedu’s gaze went to the wall.

“We crawled through a drain.”

“Ritual accounts often preserve function rather than architecture.”

“That sentence was designed to excuse anything.”

“It was designed to explain why old texts should not be read like building plans.”

Zik opened his satchel.

Balag turned.

“What are you doing?”

“Checking whether the journal has decided to become useful.”

“That thing has never been useful. It has been accurate. Those are not the same.”

Zik drew it out.

The cover was dry despite the mud and rain that had soaked the satchel. The scar along its surface caught the dawn like a thin line of oil.

He untied the wrapping.

Ninsun moved close enough to see.

Enhedu remained behind his shoulder.

Balag muttered something that sounded like a ward and might have been an insult.

Zik opened to the map.

The page showed the descending route beneath the desert. Seven narrowing turns surrounded the dark center. The line from Uruk still extended westward across the skin.

For a moment, nothing changed.

Then the ink nearest the city began to fade.

Ninsun reached for the page.

Zik caught her wrist.

“Do not touch it.”

“I know.”

“You never behave as though you know.”

The drawn outline of Uruk paled.

Walls disappeared first.

Then streets.

Then the temple platforms.

The last mark to vanish was the western gate, though they had not passed through it.

In its place, a single vertical line darkened across the route.

It ran from the top of the page to the bottom.

A threshold.

Beside it, letters appeared in Bel-iddin’s hand.

The strokes formed slowly, pressing upward through the skin as if written from the other side.

Ninsun read them aloud.

“First gate.”

Balag came closer.

“What did it take?”

Another word formed beneath the first.

Zik knew what it would say before the final mark appeared.

PAID.

The mark in his palm went cold.

Ninsun stared at the word.

“Paid with what?”

The page remained still.

Enhedu checked her knives, then the dark cloth around her wrist, then the small pouch beneath her robe.

Balag touched his throat.

Ninsun looked down at her tablets.

Zik searched himself for an absence.

He remembered his mother sleeping in the repaired house. The archive. His father’s face. Kurash raising the hammer. Uruk behind them.

Nothing seemed missing.

That did not reassure him.

Balag looked from one face to another.

“We count everything.”

“What?” Enhedu asked.

“Names. Memories. Fingers. Whatever the stories say these gates take.”

“You would notice a missing finger.”

“I would prefer to establish that.”

Ninsun pressed two fingers beneath her jaw as though checking for a pulse.

“My name is Ninsun Etana.”

“Balag,” Balag said.

Enhedu looked at them.

“This is foolish.”

“Name,” Balag said.

“You know it.”

“Say it.”

She watched him for a moment.

“Enhedu.”

Balag looked at Zik.

Zik closed the journal.

“Zikir Ashur.”

The journal knocked once between his hands.

No answer came from beneath the ground.

The sun broke over the eastern horizon.

Light spread across the fields and reached Uruk’s upper walls. The city stood behind them in brick, smoke, and gold.

It looked permanent.

That was the first lie.

Zik wrapped the journal and returned it to the satchel.

Balag finished loading the donkeys. Ninsun secured her tablets. Enhedu climbed onto the brown animal before it could object and settled the reins in one hand.

Zik mounted behind her pack frame. Balag took the gray donkey’s lead rope and walked beside it, refusing to ride until he was satisfied the load had settled evenly.

Ninsun climbed onto the remaining space behind the waterskins.

They turned east.

Behind them, Uruk disappeared one wall at a time as the land rose between the city and the desert.

Ahead, the old road ran through dead fields toward the dunes.

The journal rested silently against Zik’s ribs.

The first gate had taken its price.

None of them knew what they had paid
