CHAPTER 34

The Woman He Chose

The corridor was a lung.

It contracted and expanded around them with a slow, rhythmic wheeze of stone. Dust drifted from the ceiling in thin gray threads, caught and released with each breath the passage took. The oily stone sweated moisture that smelled of copper and wet earth, and beneath that something colder—the smell of anything in this place that had decided to remember them.

Zik walked with one hand resting on his satchel.

The journal no longer felt like a book. It felt like a hook set somewhere behind his ribs, its weight tugging with every step. Every step reminded him of the price he had paid to keep Ninsun real. Every step also reminded him that the reason for that price was gone.

He had always believed that understanding a price preserved some ownership over the transaction. He had traded sleep for profit, honesty for the Collector’s coin, comfort for survival. The City had shown him the flaw in that belief. It took the receipt along with the goods.

Behind him, Ninsun kept pace.

Her footsteps were light against the stone. For the first hour after the corridor swallowed the previous chamber he had kept expecting them to disappear—for the rhythm behind him simply to stop, the way a heartbeat stops, without announcement. He kept expecting her shape to blur, her name to slip through his fingers the way water slips through a cracked jar.

She was still there.

He knew her name–Ninsun. Nothing followed the name.

He could remember the payment.

He no longer remembered why.

Balag walked ahead, axe resting across his shoulders, the pack heavy on his back. Whatever had knocked from inside it had remained quiet since the fourth strike. He had not opened the straps. Curiosity, they had learned, was often a form of consent.

Enhedu kept to the side, the golden figurine still in her possession. She simply carried it, as though carrying it had become part of a route whose purpose belonged to someone else.

The corridor narrowed without warning.

The walls pressed close enough that Zik’s shoulders brushed both sides. Looking back had begun to feel like asking a question the City might answer with a cost. He did not look back.

After a time the walls eased. The corridor opened into a wider section scored with shallow parallel grooves, as though something large had been dragged through it more than once. The air grew colder. The copper smell thickened.

Ninsun spoke for the first time since they had left the ledger chamber.

“You don’t remember why.”

It was not a question.

“No.”

“But you remember that you chose.”

“Yes.”

A longer silence. Then: “Does that still count?”

He did not answer immediately. Some questions were themselves transactions.

“It counts to me,” he said at last.

He was not sure whether that was true.

Ninsun did not press. She walked a few paces farther and then said, almost to herself, “The tablets never mentioned this part. The part where you keep the person and lose the reason.”

Balag glanced back once. “You two finished? The floor ahead looks wrong.”

Zik tested it out of habit. His body still knew the sequence even if the conscious lesson was gone. The stone held. They continued.

The corridor began to slope more steeply. At one point the torch caught a pale scar in the limestone—the ghost of a name crossed out so thoroughly only the shape of the erasure remained. He did not stop to read it.

Behind him Ninsun’s breathing stayed steady. He counted it without meaning to: the quiet intake, the quieter release. As long as he could hear it, the payment was still holding.

He did not know how long payments held in this place.

He did not know whether the City considered the transaction closed or merely deferred.

The corridor turned.

Ahead the stone opened into a chamber whose far wall was lost in shadow. The air moved differently here—not the breathing of the passage, but a slow circulation that seemed to come from below the floor itself. Four shallow depressions marked the threshold, each the size of a kneeling figure. A fifth depression sat slightly apart, empty and darker than the rest.

Balag stopped. “This is new.”

Enhedu tilted her head, studying the darkness. “Or very old.”

Ninsun came up beside Zik. She did not touch him. She simply stood close enough that he could feel the fact of her presence without needing the memory of its value.

“You chose me,” she said quietly. Not accusing. Not grateful. Stating the remaining fact.

“I did.”

“Even without the reason.”

“Even without the reason.”

She was silent a moment. Then: “The City will not stop at one payment. You know that.”

“I know.”

“When it asks again, what will you give it?”

Zik looked into the dark beyond the chamber’s edge. The mark under the linen on his palm was warm, patient, waiting.

“I don’t know.”

They stood in silence while the City breathed around them.

Then Zik stepped into the dark, listening for Ninsun’s next breath.
