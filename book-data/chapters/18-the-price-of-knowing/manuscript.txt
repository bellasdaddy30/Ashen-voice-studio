CHAPTER 18

The Price of Knowing

No one spoke after Bel-iddin’s final words.

…is still waiting below.

The journal lay closed between them.

Rain dripped from the reed canopy into the basin beside the well. Somewhere beyond the courtyard wall a donkey brayed, followed by an argument over whether it belonged to the temple or to the man trying to drag it away.

Balag broke the silence first.

“If we’re doing this again, we leave prepared.”

Zik nodded.

“Rope. Lamps. Water. Oil.”

Ninsun gathered her copied notes.

“I’ll inventory what we already have.”

Enhedu stood.

“I’ll see what can be bought without attracting attention.”

Zik tightened the satchel across his shoulder.

“My equipment is still at my mother’s house.”

Balag looked at him.

“Then don’t wander.”

Zik almost laughed.

“You know me better than that.”

He stepped into the evening streets.

The streets were still crowded with evening traffic. Pilgrims moved toward the temples in loose, chanting groups. Merchants dragged carts through the torchlit haze. Prostitutes leaned in the shadows beneath painted doorways, calling to men who pretended not to hear them. Priests moved through the crowd with the self-important expressions of men who believed the gods had personally appointed them to supervise everyone else’s mistakes.

Normal. Manageable.

Zik preferred the city when it was loud. Noise gave a man cover. In a crowd, people saw what they expected to see: a trader, a scavenger, another hungry bastard trying to make enough silver to survive the month.

Everything had a price.

His own tools remained at his mother’s house.

He headed toward his mother’s, keeping to the shadows out of habit. Halfway there he noticed the first wrong thing.

A temple scribe was watching him from across the street.

The man was young, well-dressed, and clean enough to look suspicious on principle. His linen robe was too fine for the neighborhood, and the wax tablet tucked beneath his arm was decorated with a small silver seal. He was the kind of man who usually never looked twice at men like Zik.

But he was looking now.

Zik kept walking.

The scribe kept watching.

Zik slowed near a stall selling bronze chisels. The scribe stopped.

Zik turned left.

The scribe followed.

Zik did not look back again. He changed direction twice, moving through the evening crowd with the casual rhythm of a man who had nowhere particular to be. He passed a spice seller, crossed behind a row of pottery stalls, and slipped into a narrow alley between a wine merchant’s warehouse and a shuttered bakery.

The alley smelled of old water and rotting grain. Zik waited.

The scribe entered thirty seconds later.

Zik stepped out behind him and pressed a knife lightly against his side.

“Temple business or personal?” Zik asked.

The scribe stiffened. “Neither.”

“That wasn’t one of the options.”

The young man swallowed. Zik pressed the blade a little harder. Not enough to cut. Enough to remind him that the distinction was temporary.

“The Collector sends his regards,” the scribe said.

“Then send mine back.”

“He would like to speak with you again.”

“Tell him I’m busy.”

“Tonight.”

Zik smiled faintly.

“That sounded less like a request.”

The scribe glanced toward the mouth of the alley. No one was there. The crowd moved past without looking in.

“The Collector is prepared to offer ten times what you received today.”

“For the beads?”

The scribe hesitated.

Zik’s smile disappeared.

“For the journal,” the scribe said.

The knife stopped moving.

He continued quickly. “Or anything else you removed from the chamber.”

For a moment the alley seemed to contract around them. Zik had never mentioned the journal. He had never told the Collector about the chamber beneath the desert. He had never told him about the skeleton. He had never told him about the symbol appearing beneath his skin.

The scribe knew about all of it. Or enough of it.

“How does he know?” Zik asked.

The scribe’s mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Zik turned the knife slightly.

“Try again.”

“The Collector knows many things.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“He says the mark has a way of announcing itself.”

Zik looked down at his palm. The symbol was barely visible beneath the skin—a faint discoloration, an old scar if a man didn’t know what he was looking at.

“Announcing itself to whom?”

The scribe looked away.

“Especially to those who have seen it before.”

The words settled between them. Zik slowly removed the knife.

The scribe turned to face him. “Is that all?”

“No.”

Zik waited.

The scribe’s face had gone pale.

“The Collector says you should not go back.”

Zik stared at him.

“To the site?”

The scribe said nothing. That was answer enough.

Zik stepped closer. “Did he tell you why?”

The scribe’s eyes flicked toward the knife.

“No.”

“Did he tell you what happens if I do?”

“No.”

“Then you have wasted both our time.”

Zik stepped aside.

The scribe hurried past him, then stopped at the end of the alley.

“Zikir.”

Zik looked over his shoulder. The scribe’s face had changed. The nervousness was gone. For just a second he looked older.

“Some doors are sealed because what is behind them is dangerous.”

Zik waited.

The scribe’s eyes dropped to the mark on Zik’s palm.

“Some are sealed because the thing behind them is still waiting.”

Then he walked away.

Zik remained in the alley.

The words should have sounded like a threat. Instead they sounded familiar. He hated that.

When Zik returned to his mother’s house to gather the last of his gear, he found the second wrong thing.

The door was locked.

The crack in the frame was exactly where he had left it. Nothing looked disturbed.

That was the problem.

Zik stood in the hallway for nearly a minute without entering.

His father had taught him to notice things that were about to fail. He had also taught him that a room could tell you when someone had been inside it. A moved object. A displaced grain of dust. A door closed with the wrong amount of force.

Zik pushed the door open.

The room was dark.

His mother was asleep.

The small clay tablet he used as a weight for papers was on the table. It had been beside the window.

His spare knife was beneath his pallet. It had been on the shelf.

His father’s measuring cord still hung beside the doorway.

Bel-iddin had always coiled it clockwise.

Someone had hung it back the other way.

Nobody else would have noticed.

Zik did.

And the loose brick behind which he kept emergency coin was slightly askew.

Zik closed the door behind him.

He did not wake his mother.

He crossed the room and crouched beside the wall. The brick had been replaced carefully. Too carefully. He pulled it free.

The emergency coins were still there. He counted them anyway.

One. Two. Three.

All present.

He put them back and examined the brick. A small smear of pale dust marked one edge. Someone had removed it. Someone had put it back. Someone had been careful enough to leave the money untouched.

Zik sat on the edge of his pallet for a long moment.

His mother breathed softly in her sleep. The kiln beneath the house gave a low, constant rumble. The room was warm. Familiar. Safe.

The word was ridiculous.

Zik looked at the journal in his hands.

The mark on his palm throbbed in time with his heartbeat. He had lied to the Collector. But the Collector had already known he was lying. That was the part Zik couldn’t fit into the arithmetic.

A lie was a transaction. You gave someone a falsehood and received something in return: information, time, distance, safety.

But the Collector had not been negotiating with him. He had been waiting.

Zik opened the journal. The pages were blank. He turned to the map.

The vertical section was still there. The rivers. The impossible structures. The spiral descending through the layers of the world.

At the bottom of the map, beneath the words I WAS WRONG ABOUT THE DEPTH, there was now a second line.

Zik stared at it.

He had not seen it before.

The handwriting was his father’s.

I WAS WRONG ABOUT WHO WAS LOOKING.

He read it twice.

Then a third time.

Not because he doubted the words.

Because he was trying to remember whether they had existed a heartbeat earlier.

They hadn’t.

Zik’s breath stopped.

He looked toward the sleeping pallet. His mother had not moved.

The house was silent except for the kiln. He looked back at the journal.

The ink beneath the sentence was still wet.

Zik touched it.

The mark in his palm burned. He pulled his hand away.

Zik closed the journal.

He closed the journal carefully.

The leather settled beneath his hand.

His mother still slept.

The kiln rumbled beneath the floor exactly as it had every night since he was a child.

Nothing inside the room had changed.

Everything outside it had.

Zik slung the satchel over his shoulder.

At the doorway he paused.

He almost woke her.

Instead he repaired the cracked frame with two careful pushes of his thumb until the wood sat flush again.

Habit.

Then he stepped into the alley.

The city breathed around him.

Torchlight.

Voices.

Wheels.

Dogs.

Somewhere, someone laughed.

Halfway to the street the journal moved.

Once.

Not the shifting of paper.

A knock.

Zik stopped.

The mark beneath the linen pulsed once.

Silence.

Then, from somewhere deep beneath Uruk, something answered.

Not loudly.

Not with sound.

With recognition.

Zik stood motionless in the alley.

He looked toward the dark western quarter where the others would be waiting before dawn.

Then back toward his mother’s house.

The journal became still.

It did not feel asleep.

It felt as though it had received an answer.
