CHAPTER 20

Surveyor of the Lower Thresholds

“Zik.”

The Collector’s voice came through the shutter.

Zik kept his hand pressed against the journal.

The leather beneath his palm was motionless, but the mark in his skin tightened as though someone had drawn a cord through it.

Balag shifted beside the wall. His axe came free without scraping the plaster.

Enhedu raised one finger, warning him to wait.

Ninsun stood beside the table with Bel-iddin’s warning folded against her chest. In the darkness, the pale clay strip in her other hand looked like a shard of bone.

Zik moved toward the shutter.

“You could have used the door.”

“I did,” the Collector said. “It remains barred.”

“That was intentional.”

“So I assumed.”

His voice sounded close enough for his breath to touch the wood, yet Zik had heard no approach after the second footstep. No robe brushing the wall. No sandal shifting in the wet grit below the window.

Zik stopped an arm’s length from the shutter.

“How did you find us?”

“I have known where this room was since you rented it.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No. It was the answer available to you.”

Enhedu glanced at Zik and shaped a silent word with her lips.

Alone?

Zik shook his head once. He did not know.

The Collector continued.

“You found the seventh drawer.”

Not a question.

Zik looked toward Ninsun.

Her expression sharpened.

“Mardu told you?” Zik asked.

“Mardu tells me nothing. It is one of his better qualities.”

“You have people in the archive.”

“I have people everywhere men mistake employment for loyalty.”

Balag took one silent step toward the shutter.

Zik raised a hand.

“What was inside when my father opened it?”

The rain whispered along the roof tiles.

For several breaths, the Collector said nothing.

Then:

“His name.”

Zik’s hand tightened around the strap of his satchel.

“What did the record call him?”

“Surveyor of the Lower Thresholds.”

The title entered the room and seemed to take up space.

Ninsun lowered the strip of clay.

“He was not,” Zik said.

“Records disagree.”

“My father surveyed irrigation channels, burial chambers, collapsed foundations, and whatever else people were foolish enough to build over unstable ground. He was never appointed to anything called a lower threshold.”

“No appointment was required.”

“You just said there was a record.”

“Yes.”

“That is usually what appointments are for.”

“Usually.”

Ninsun approached the shutter.

“The tablet was older than Bel-iddin.”

The Collector did not answer immediately.

“Yes.”

“How much older?”

“Old enough that the scribe who impressed it had no reason to know his name.”

Balag’s grip tightened on the haft of his axe.

Zik leaned nearer the wood.

“What else did it say?”

“The date of his appointment.”

“When?”

“The day Kurash died.”

The words reached Balag before they reached anyone else.

His shoulders went still.

“That happened years later,” Zik said.

“I know.”

“You saw the tablet?”

“I saw your father break it.”

Balag crossed half the room before Zik caught his forearm.

The movement was silent except for the faint creak of leather around the axe grip.

“Why?” Zik asked.

“Because the final line named his successor.”

The journal shifted beneath Zik’s hand.

Not a knock.

A slow pressure from inside the satchel.

“Who?” he asked.

The Collector waited.

Zik heard water dripping from the eaves into the alley below.

One drop.

Then another.

“You,” the Collector said.

The journal knocked once.

The sound was softer than it had been in the courtyard.

That made it worse.

No one moved.

Zik felt the impact through his palm, into his wrist, and along the bones of his arm.

Balag looked at the satchel.

Ninsun’s eyes had gone to the mark beneath Zik’s skin.

Enhedu watched the shutter.

“What does successor mean?” Zik asked.

“It means Bel-iddin spent the rest of his life trying to keep you away from that tomb.”

Zik almost laughed.

The sound never reached his throat.

“He brought me there.”

“He believed distance would not be enough.”

“So he took me closer?”

“He believed knowledge might teach you caution.”

“That suggests he did not know me very well.”

“He knew you better than you think.”

“Then he should have known caution works poorly when paired with secrets.”

The Collector’s fingers touched the outside of the shutter.

Zik saw their shadows through the narrow gap between the boards.

Long fingers.

Bare.

No rings.

“He tried other methods,” the Collector said.

“What methods?”

“He altered records. He paid men to forget routes. He moved objects from one collection to another. He convinced a priesthood that a damaged seal belonged in a provincial storehouse where no one would bother cataloguing it for three generations.”

Ninsun’s mouth tightened at the insult to cataloguing.

“He erased Ammaru?” she asked.

“No.”

“Who did?”

“That question is older than your profession.”

“My profession is older than most kingdoms.”

“That is what archivists say whenever they want to make dust sound important.”

Ninsun stepped closer to the shutter.

“Open it.”

Enhedu caught the back of her robe.

“No.”

“I only want to see his face.”

“That has killed better people than you.”

Ninsun looked offended.

“I doubt that.”

Zik kept his voice low.

“My father knew the seventh drawer named me.”

“Yes.”

“He knew before Kurash died?”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly.

Zik noticed.

“So the tablet changed.”

Silence.

Balag looked at him.

Ninsun stopped resisting Enhedu’s grip.

Zik moved closer to the shutter until his breath touched the wood.

“It changed,” he repeated. “The date appeared after Kurash died.”

The Collector’s shadow remained still.

“That is one interpretation.”

“It is the only one you are trying not to say.”

“The tablet was old.”

“So was the journal. That hasn’t prevented it from writing new sentences.”

The mark in Zik’s palm pulsed.

For a moment, the Collector’s fingers on the other side of the shutter flexed.

A tiny movement.

Recognition.

Zik saw it.

“You’ve seen the journal write,” he said.

“I have seen many things that should have remained impossible.”

“That isn’t a denial.”

“No.”

“What did it write for you?”

The fingers withdrew.

Rain struck the shutter where they had been.

When the Collector answered, his voice had moved slightly to the left.

“Nothing.”

“You’re lying.”

“Of course.”

Enhedu tilted her head, listening.

“He’s moving,” she whispered.

Balag stepped toward the wall beside the window, preparing to strike through the shutter if the Collector tried to open it.

Zik did not take his eyes from the dark line between the boards.

“And now?” he asked. “What does successor mean now?”

“Now the journal has drawn you a road back.”

“And you sent a scribe to tell me not to follow it.”

“I sent a scribe because approaching you directly would have been noticed.”

“By whom?”

“That is not a useful question.”

“It is becoming one.”

“The useful question is why you believed the man was mine.”

Zik said nothing.

The Collector’s voice seemed to recede another finger’s breadth from the shutter.

“The seal on his tablet?” he continued. “The message? The mention of the mark?”

Zik remembered the young man’s face changing at the mouth of the alley.

The nervousness disappearing.

The sudden age in his eyes.

Some doors are sealed because the thing behind them is still waiting.

“You didn’t send him,” Zik said.

Ninsun looked toward him.

Balag’s eyes narrowed.

“No,” the Collector said.

The room seemed to contract.

“Who was he?”

“I hoped you might tell me.”

“He knew about the journal.”

“Yes.”

“The chamber.”

“Probably.”

“The mark.”

“Certainly.”

“And he used your name.”

“Most thieves use something that belongs to another man.”

Enhedu moved closer to the shutter.

“You let him walk away?”

Zik looked at her.

“He looked like a temple scribe.”

“That is not an answer.”

“He also had a knife against his side.”

“Apparently that did not inconvenience him.”

The Collector gave a faint sound through the wood.

It might have been laughter.

Enhedu’s knife struck the shutter.

The blade passed between the boards and stopped.

There was no cry outside.

No sudden movement.

She withdrew it.

The point was clean.

“You are standing farther away than you sound,” she said.

“Yes.”

“How?”

“Practice.”

“I dislike him,” she said to no one in particular.

“That may be the safest conclusion we have reached,” Ninsun said.

Zik kept his attention on the alley.

“Why are you here?”

“Because you are leaving through the lime-kiln breach.”

Enhedu’s second knife appeared in her other hand.

Balag looked toward Zik.

Ninsun folded Bel-iddin’s warning once more.

Zik touched the shutter.

“How do you know that?”

“It is the exit Bel-iddin used.”

“I never told my father about it.”

“He knew the city before you learned to steal from it.”

“I do not steal from cities.”

“You have made a profession of removing their contents.”

“Only the unattended ones.”

“History is unattended property to you.”

“History is property to everyone. I am simply less dishonest about the transfer.”

“Is the breach watched?” Balag asked.

The Collector did not answer him.

Zik repeated the question.

“Everything is watched,” the Collector said. “The useful question is whether the watcher expects to be seen.”

“Who is watching it?”

“I do not know.”

“You know where we are, what route we planned to take, what we found in the archive, and what my father broke twenty years ago.”

“Your confidence in me is touching.”

“But you do not know who is watching a hole in a wall.”

“A man who claims to know everything is selling religion.”

“And what are you selling?”

“Time.”

Something scraped softly beneath the shutter.

Every weapon in the room shifted toward the sound.

A small object slid through the gap and fell onto the floor.

It struck the plaster once, turned on its edge, and settled.

Bronze.

Balag placed one foot over it before anyone else moved.

“Step back,” Enhedu said.

“I am already standing.”

“From the object.”

“It weighs less than a date.”

“Dates rarely contain poison needles.”

“Your life has been badly organized.”

He crouched without lowering his axe and picked it up between two fingers.

A seal.

It was no larger than the first joint of his thumb, darkened with age and worn smooth around the rim. Seven narrow lines descended along its face.

They did not meet at the center.

The center had been left empty.

Balag held it toward the roof vent.

Ninsun came closer despite Enhedu’s hand on her sleeve.

“No inscription,” she said.

“Not on that side,” the Collector answered.

Balag turned it over.

The reverse bore a shallow pattern of nested angles. At first they looked decorative. Then Zik saw that each angle corresponded to a turn in the descending route drawn by the journal.

Ninsun saw it at the same moment.

“It is a key.”

“No,” the Collector said. “A key enters a lock.”

“What does this do?”

“It tells the door what stands before it.”

Balag held the seal farther from himself.

“That answer improved nothing.”

“What mistake?” Zik asked.

The Collector was silent.

“You called it a mistake.”

“A mistake I have carried for twenty years.”

Zik looked at the seal.

“What did you use it on?”

“The black floor.”

The room lost what little warmth it had retained.

Balag’s gaze went to the warning page in Ninsun’s hand.

Do not strike the black floor.

“What happened?” Zik asked.

“The floor remembered me.”

Ninsun frowned.

“That is not an archaeological description.”

“No,” the Collector said. “It is the only accurate one.”

“Did it open?”

“Not in the way I intended.”

“What way did it open?”

The Collector’s voice hardened.

“Place the seal against the lower door. Not the black floor.”

Zik heard the change.

Not fear exactly.

The memory of it.

Ninsun took the seal from Balag and studied the edges.

“There is residue in the cuts.”

“Do not scrape it.”

She paused with one fingernail already against the bronze.

“Why?”

“Because some of it is mine.”

Ninsun lowered her hand.

Enhedu looked at the seal.

“I dislike the object now as well.”

“That saves time,” Balag said.

Zik pressed closer to the shutter.

“Why give it to us?”

“Because Bel-iddin took the other one.”

“Other?”

“There were two.”

“What happened to his?”

“He carried it into the tomb.”

“And brought it back?”

“No.”

Zik felt the journal move again.

A slow tightening against the satchel’s leather, as though it had heard something it wanted.

“My father returned from the tomb.”

“Yes.”

“Then why didn’t the seal?”

The Collector did not answer.

Zik waited.

Rainwater ran beneath the shutter and formed a narrow crescent on the floor.

“Did he leave it below?” Ninsun asked.

“He believed he did.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

“Where is it now?”

The Collector’s footsteps began to recede.

One.

Then another.

Zik lifted the shutter.

Enhedu caught his wrist.

“Wait.”

He pulled free and shoved the wooden panel outward.

The alley was empty.

Rain silvered the stones. Water dripped from the awnings. A dog stood beneath a stairway at the far end, watching him with the offended expression of an animal disturbed during private business.

No Collector.

No robes disappearing around the corner.

No ripples in the puddles except those made by rain.

Zik leaned farther out.

The wall opposite the room bore a line of wet handprints.

They began beneath the shutter.

Five fingers.

Then another print slightly higher.

Then another.

They climbed the wall vertically until they reached the roofline.

Zik stared.

Enhedu looked over his shoulder.

“He did not leave through the alley.”

“No.”

Balag joined them.

“Did he climb?”

Enhedu examined the prints.

“The fingers point downward.”

Ninsun came to the window with the bronze seal in her palm.

The handprints ended at the roof.

No marks led across the tiles.

Balag stepped back from the shutter.

“I dislike him more than before.”

Zik closed it.

“That still may be the safest conclusion we have reached.”

Ninsun placed the bronze seal on the table.

Rainwater had gathered along the grooves.

It did not spill when she tilted it.

The droplets clung inside the seven lines, each one held in place as though the metal had become a tiny descending channel.

“The lower door,” she said.

“If there is one,” Balag answered.

“There is.”

“You have not seen it.”

“The map shows it.”

“The map draws itself inside a book made from a dead man.”

“Yes.”

“You continue saying things as though they support your position.”

“They do.”

Zik took the seal.

The metal was colder than the room.

The instant it touched his marked palm, heat ran through his wrist.

He nearly dropped it.

The seven lines on the bronze darkened.

Not all at once.

One after another, from the rim toward the empty center.

Ninsun leaned closer.

“What did you do?”

“Nothing.”

“That is becoming your most destructive habit.”

The final line darkened.

The mark beneath Zik’s skin answered with a pulse.

For one breath, the empty center of the seal reflected a room that was not theirs.

Black walls.

A low ceiling.

A narrow door standing open.

Beyond it, something pale moved out of sight.

Then the bronze showed only bronze again.

Zik closed his fingers around it.

“What did you see?” Enhedu asked.

He looked at her.

“A door.”

“That was already implied by the conversation.”

“It was open.”

Balag tightened the rope around one of the packs.

“Then we should leave before whatever opened it reaches the stairs.”

They finished packing.

No one argued.

Balag divided the weight among them with the authority of a man who expected everyone else to make poor decisions if given a choice. He tied one rope across his chest and another beneath his pack. The hammer went at his side, wrapped in cloth so it would not strike the chisels while they moved.

Ninsun secured her tablets between folded robes and wrapped the wet clay strip in linen.

“It will deform,” Balag said.

“Not if you stop sitting on my records.”

“I sat on one tablet.”

“You shattered the corner.”

“It was already damaged.”

“It had survived six centuries.”

“It had not met me.”

Enhedu checked the room for anything they had touched.

She wiped the table, the wall cavity, the shutter latch, and the roof edge with a strip of damp cloth.

Zik wrapped the journal twice and placed it at the bottom of his satchel.

The leather knocked once against his palm as he lowered it.

A small, patient impact.

He stopped.

Balag looked over.

“Again?”

Zik tightened the wrapping.

“No.”

The lie convinced no one.

He put the bronze seal into a separate pouch and tied it around his neck. The metal rested against his chest, colder than the rain.

Ninsun handed him Bel-iddin’s warning.

“You keep it.”

“Why?”

“He wrote it for you.”

“He wrote it for Kurash.”

“He wrote it because he knew Kurash would not listen to him.”

Balag’s face changed.

Only slightly.

Ninsun saw and softened her voice.

“That was not blame.”

“It sounded familiar enough.”

Zik took the page.

The papyrus was thin where Bel-iddin’s stylus had pressed too hard beneath Kurash’s name.

He folded it and placed it beside the seal.

They left through the roof rather than the door.

Enhedu went first, lowering herself onto the adjoining tiles. Ninsun followed with Balag’s hand around her forearm. Zik passed the packs across one at a time.

When Balag climbed out last, the room behind them remained dark.

The shutter shifted inward after he released it.

Not closing.

Breathing.

Balag stared at it.

“Did you see that?”

“Yes,” Zik said.

“Good.”

“Why is that good?”

“I am tired of being the only one who sees impossible things.”

They crossed the roofs.

The rain stopped while they moved through the western quarter. Clouds covered the moon, and water streamed from the buildings into the lanes below.

They descended behind a dyer’s workshop.

The vats had been covered for the night, but the air still smelled of indigo, lye, and wet wool. Zik landed beside a stack of clay jars and caught one before it tipped.

Enhedu dropped lightly beside him.

Ninsun descended with less elegance and more commentary.

Balag came last, dislodging a section of reed matting that struck him across the shoulders.

He pulled it free.

“The city objects to my shape.”

“The city has standards,” Enhedu said.

They entered the street.

Uruk had emptied to guards, late porters, and dogs searching the gutters. A line of temple laborers crossed ahead carrying covered baskets between them. None looked toward Zik’s group.

They kept to the narrow lanes.

At every junction, Enhedu moved ahead, listened, then signaled them through. Once she stopped so suddenly that Ninsun walked into Balag’s back.

Two guards stood beneath an awning twenty paces ahead.

One held a lamp.

The other had a tablet tucked beneath his arm.

Zik looked at the silver seal fixed to its edge.

The same seal the false scribe had carried.

The mark in his palm began to itch.

Enhedu drew them into the doorway of a closed grain shop.

The guards did not speak.

They simply watched the street.

Rainwater dripped from the lamp guard’s helmet.

His companion turned his head.

Not toward the alley.

Toward Zik.

The distance was too great for him to see through the darkness.

Still, his face aligned exactly with the doorway.

The journal pressed against Zik’s back.

Once.

The guard smiled.

Enhedu pulled Zik deeper into the recess.

The guards continued walking.

Their sandals passed the entrance without slowing.

Neither looked inside.

Balag waited until the sound faded.

“Collector’s men?”

“No,” Zik said.

“How do you know?”

“He said he did not send the scribe.”

“You believe him?”

“About that.”

“Why?”

Zik looked at the wet street where the guards had vanished.

“Because they wanted me to know they had seen me.”

Ninsun adjusted her pack.

“Then we should assume the breach is watched.”

“We already assumed that.”

“No,” she said. “We discussed it. Assumption requires discipline.”

Balag looked at Zik.

“Do you understand why she annoys me?”

“Completely.”

Ninsun started walking.

“Both of you are carrying my water.”

The old lime kilns stood abandoned near the city wall.

Their collapsed mouths opened from the ground like broken ovens. Rain had turned the ash around them into pale mud that clung to sandals and gave off a bitter mineral smell.

The city wall rose behind them, black against the clouded sky.

Zik led the others between two ruined firing chambers and stopped beneath the remains of a brick arch.

Nothing moved.

No lamp.

No guard.

No false temple scribe standing politely in the rain.

Enhedu crouched and touched the mud.

“Tracks.”

“How many?” Balag asked.

“Too many.”

“Recent?”

“The rain has softened them.”

She moved two fingers along a shallow impression.

“One person stood here for some time.”

“Waiting?” Ninsun asked.

“Or watching.”

Zik studied the kiln.

The thorn brush concealing the drainage channel had been replaced.

He knew because the longest branch usually pointed toward the wall.

Now it pointed outward.

“They found the breach,” he said.

Balag shifted the axe higher.

“Then we use another exit.”

“The nearest is beneath a tannery.”

“How far?”

“Too far.”

“Watched?”

“Almost certainly.”

Enhedu examined the brush without touching it.

“No cord. No bell. No blade.”

“That you can see,” Ninsun said.

Enhedu looked at her.

“I was beginning to like you.”

“No, you were not.”

“No.”

Zik crouched beside the opening.

The mud at its edge held a single clean impression.

Seven narrow lines around an empty center.

The same pattern as the seal hanging beneath his robe.

He touched it.

The mark in his palm pulsed.

The impression softened immediately, collapsing into featureless mud.

Balag saw.

“What was it?”

“Permission.”

“From whom?”

Zik pulled the thorn brush aside.

The drainage channel gaped beneath it.

“Let’s avoid finding out.”

They lowered the packs through first.

Enhedu entered on her stomach and vanished into the dark.

Ninsun followed, pushing her satchel ahead of her.

Zik knelt at the mouth.

The passage smelled of wet ash and stagnant water. Cold air moved through it from beyond the wall.

Balag looked at the opening, then at his own shoulders.

“You have used this before?”

“Once.”

“Were you smaller?”

“Yes.”

“That would have been useful information.”

“You would still have followed.”

“I would have complained earlier.”

“You have compensated.”

Zik crawled into the channel.

Mud soaked through his sleeves immediately.

The ceiling pressed close above his back. Water ran around his hands and pooled beneath his knees. Ahead, Ninsun’s sandals disappeared around a bend in the drain.

Behind him, Balag dragged the thorn brush back across the opening.

Darkness closed.

They moved on hands and knees beneath the city wall, pushing their equipment ahead.

Every sound enlarged inside the channel.

Breath.

Cloth scraping brick.

The soft knock of a waterskin against stone.

Zik felt the journal shift whenever his pack struck the ceiling.

Not random movement.

A rhythm.

Once.

Pause.

Twice.

Pause.

Once.

He stopped.

Balag’s hand struck his boot.

“Why?”

“Listen.”

They held still.

Water trickled through the passage.

From somewhere beneath them came a faint answering knock.

Once.

Pause.

Twice.

Ninsun looked back over her shoulder.

“There is another channel below us.”

“No,” Zik said.

“How do you know?”

“The old drainage plans show only this one.”

“You trust the city records now?”

Another knock came from beneath the floor.

Closer.

Balag pressed one palm to the brick.

The sound stopped.

He withdrew his hand.

It began again.

Once.

Pause.

Twice.

Enhedu’s voice came from ahead.

“Move.”

No one argued.

They crawled faster.

The channel narrowed near the center of the wall. Balag’s pack scraped both sides at once. He cursed, twisted, and forced himself through by exhaling until his chest flattened enough to pass.

Zik’s fingers struck something hard in the mud.

He pulled it free.

A fragment of fired clay.

Curved.

Inscribed.

He held it near the tiny glow of Ninsun’s covered lamp.

One sign remained visible.

A descending wedge.

Then another.

Then another.

A fourth had been added beneath them.

The same message they had found in the wall cavity.

Zik dropped the fragment.

The water carried it backward toward Balag.

“Why did you do that?” Ninsun asked.

“Because I have enough messages.”

“That was an artifact.”

“It was following us.”

“Artifacts do not follow people.”

The clay fragment stopped moving against the current.

Then slid toward them.

Ninsun stared.

“Leave it,” she said.

They moved.

At the far end, cold air touched Zik’s face.

Enhedu climbed out first.

She did not speak.

Ninsun pushed her pack toward the opening, and Enhedu pulled it through. Then Ninsun disappeared after it.

Zik crawled the last few cubits.

Hands caught his wrists.

Enhedu hauled him onto damp earth beyond Uruk.

The air outside the walls felt wider and colder. The desert smelled of wet dust, thorn, and distant salt.

Zik rolled aside.

Balag forced himself through the opening with a curse that continued long after his shoulders were free. He stood, brushing mud from his robe and beard.

Behind them, the city wall rose black against the clouded sky.

A line of torches moved along its upper walkway.

None stopped above the breach.

Ahead, the desert stretched beyond the reach of their covered lamps.

Enhedu stood facing east.

Ninsun checked the clay tablets before checking herself.

Balag tightened the rope across his chest.

Zik lifted his pack.

The journal remained still.

Not asleep.

Waiting.

Whatever had answered beneath Uruk had not answered again.

He looked back at the wall.

For an instant, a figure stood atop it.

Too tall.

Too narrow.

Motionless between two torches.

Then the flames guttered, and the figure was gone.

Balag followed Zik’s gaze.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That answer,” Ninsun said, “has become exhausting.”

Zik turned east.

Balag looked toward the unseen tomb.

“How far before we stop?”

“We don’t,” Zik said. “Not until sunrise.”

Enhedu adjusted the knife at her belt.

“And after sunrise?”

Zik touched the bronze seal beneath his robe.

The seven lines felt warm now.

He looked into the dark.

“We find out how deep my father was wrong.”

They started walking.
